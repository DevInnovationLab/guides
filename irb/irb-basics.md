## IRB Basics

<div class="ref-summary" markdown="1">
This page explains what an Institutional Review Board (IRB) is, why it matters for the Lab's work, and how to tell whether what you are doing counts as human subjects research. It is meant to build understanding rather than walk you through a submission. For the mechanics of drafting and submitting a protocol, see the protocol how-to guide.
</div>

<hr class="section-divider">

## What an IRB is

An Institutional Review Board is an administrative body that protects the welfare and rights of the human subjects who choose to participate in research conducted by an institution. At the University of Chicago, social science research is reviewed by the Social and Behavioral Sciences (SBS) IRB, and submissions are handled through AURA.

An IRB does three things that matter to you in practice:

1. It reviews your plan before you start, and asks whether the risks to participants are reasonable relative to what the research will produce.
2. It holds you to that plan once approved, which is why changes to design, instruments, sample, or partners generally require an amendment.
3. It creates a documented record that the institution reviewed and authorized the work.

An IRB is not an ethics consultant, a project manager, or a data quality reviewer. It will not tell you whether your study is a good idea.

<hr class="section-divider">

## Start with a scenario

> You are leading fieldwork for a study on agricultural development in rural areas of a developing country. The fieldwork is run by a survey firm.
>
> An enumerator from the firm approaches the household head, an elderly woman working in a field. He introduces himself and says, "We are here to collect information about you and your farm for research," and then begins asking survey questions and recording her responses on a tablet.
{: .example}

What is wrong here?

<details markdown="1">
<summary>Think it through, then expand</summary>

The woman never consented. She was told what the enumerator was doing, but she was not told:

* who is running the research and who is funding it
* what will happen to her answers, who will see them, and how long they will be kept
* what the risks and benefits are, if any
* that she may decline to answer any question, or stop at any point, with no consequence
* how to reach someone if she has questions or complaints later

Being informed is not the same as consenting. Consent requires that she understand the above and then agree, voluntarily, before data collection begins. A power imbalance makes this sharper: an unannounced visitor with an institutional affiliation and a tablet can feel like someone who should not be refused.

Notice also what is not the fix. Adding an IRB approval number to the enumerator's script would not have made this exchange ethical. The protocol has to be implemented, and the field team has to be trained and monitored so that it is.
</details>

## Compliance is not the same as ethics

The most common misreading of IRB review is treating approval as proof that a study is ethical. Evans ([2024](#sources)) puts the limitation directly:

> IRBs have the function of ensuring that research that involves "human subjects" (i.e. people) is carried out ethically. However, clearance from an IRB is not a substitute for engaging directly with ethical issues in RCTs and other research (O'Flynn, Barnett, and Camfield 2016). As Watima wrote, "Being compliant does not mean it's ethical" (Watima 2020). Some IRBs in high-income countries may have little familiarity with conditions in low-income environments, and IRBs and social science review boards in both high and low income environments are heterogeneous in quality across countries and institutions (Cronin-Furman and Lake 2018). But engaging with IRBs still provides an important layer of oversight for research that studies people and their actions.

Two things follow. First, an IRB reviewing a study in Madhya Pradesh from Chicago may not recognize context-specific risks, which is part of why local review and local collaborators matter. Second, the questions an IRB does not ask remain yours to answer: whether the study should be run at all, whether the knowledge gained justifies the burden placed on respondents, and whether people excluded from a treatment arm are being treated fairly.

The useful summary: **IRB approval is necessary but not sufficient.**

## Why this matters for the Lab

The consequences of a weak or inaccurate protocol are concrete and they compound.

**For the project.** Fieldwork cannot begin without approval, and an incomplete submission adds review cycles at the point where the launch calendar is least flexible. Data collected outside the scope of an approved protocol may be unusable, which means resources spent and nothing to analyze. Journals increasingly ask for the approving body and the approval number.

**For the Lab.** Repeated problems with submissions slow down every subsequent protocol the Lab files. Where DIL works through implementing partners and government counterparts, a compliance failure damages relationships that took years to build and that other projects rely on.

**For the PIs.** The principal investigator is accountable for the conduct of the study, including work carried out by survey firms and partners. Serious deviations can trigger suspension of a study and reporting obligations at the institutional level.

## What counts as human subjects research

Three criteria have to be met. Work through them in order.

### 1. Is the project research?


[The Common Rule](https://www.hhs.gov/ohrp/regulations-and-policy/regulations/45-cfr-46/index.html) defines research as a systematic investigation, including research development, testing, and evaluation, designed to develop or contribute to generalizable knowledge.

The phrase that does the work is *generalizable knowledge*. Internal monitoring meant only to improve one partner's own program delivery is usually not research. A study designed to produce findings that inform policy or practice beyond that one program usually is. Intent at the design stage matters more than where the output eventually appears.

### 2. Does the research involve human subjects?


A human subject is a living individual about whom an investigator conducting research either:

* obtains information or biospecimens through intervention or interaction with the individual, and uses, studies, or analyzes the information or biospecimens; or
* obtains, uses, studies, analyzes, or generates identifiable private information or identifiable biospecimens

Two paths, and either one is enough. The first covers surveys, interviews, focus groups, experiments, and observation. The second covers secondary data work with no contact whatsoever, when the information is private and identifiable.

"Identifiable" is broader than names. Geolocation, phone numbers, household IDs that can be linked back to a roster, small-cell demographic combinations, and photographs can all identify someone. Ask whether an individual could be re-identified with reasonable effort, not whether a name field exists.

### 3. Is UChicago engaged in research involving human subjects?

An institution is engaged when its personnel do things like intervene with participants, interact with them for research purposes, obtain identifiable private information, or, in many arrangements, design the study and direct the data collection that a partner carries out.

This is the criterion that gets missed. Receiving only deidentified data does not automatically place the Lab outside the scope of review when the Lab shaped what was collected and from whom.

## Self-checks: human subjects research or not?

Reason through each one against the three criteria before expanding. In real cases the determination is made by the IRB, not by us; the point of the exercise is to know when to ask.

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Scenario 1.</b> We are partnering with PxD on a research project. PxD is working with a state government to collect data from farmers on their agricultural practices through phone and in-person surveys. We are co-designing the surveys and the study with PxD. We will receive deidentified data from PxD and analyze it.</summary>

**Treat this as human subjects research and submit it.**

* *Research?* Yes. A designed study intended to produce generalizable findings.
* *Human subjects?* Yes. Living individuals are being surveyed and their responses analyzed.
* *UChicago engaged?* This is the live question, and co-designing the surveys and the study is what tips it. The Lab is shaping the interaction with participants, not simply receiving a dataset that already existed. Deidentification at the point of transfer limits the Lab's exposure to identifiable information, but it does not undo the Lab's role in generating the data.

The trap to avoid: "we only touch deidentified data, so we are not engaged." Engagement follows from your role in the research, not only from which columns land in your folder. Where the Lab genuinely does nothing but reanalyze an existing deidentified dataset it had no hand in creating, the answer can differ, which is exactly why the determination goes to the IRB rather than to the analyst.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Scenario 2.</b> We are conducting focus group discussions and qualitative interviews with farmers in Madhya Pradesh to understand whether they have experienced drought in the last two years and how their households responded to low rainfall, including distressed asset sales and education investment choices. We will collect geolocations but not names.</summary>

**Yes, human subjects research.**

* *Research?* Yes. Qualitative design and the absence of a treatment arm change nothing here; the investigation is systematic and aimed at generalizable knowledge.
* *Human subjects?* Yes, on both paths. There is direct interaction, and geolocation is identifiable private information even with no name field, since a GPS point at household resolution identifies a household.
* *UChicago engaged?* Yes. Lab personnel are running the interaction.

Two further points worth noticing. Focus groups carry a confidentiality limit you cannot engineer away: participants hear each other, so the protocol has to address what is said in the room, not only what the research team records. And distressed asset sales and schooling decisions are sensitive topics in a small community, which raises the standard for how identifiers and transcripts are stored and who may see them.
</details>

<details markdown="1">
<summary><b>Scenario 3.</b> We are conducting detailed interviews with pump operators and households in rural Odisha. We are working with the state government of Odisha to identify challenges facing their ILC operations in eastern parts of the state. We will collect PII from the households and test household water supply.</summary>

**Yes, human subjects research, and the clearest case of the three.**

* *Research?* Yes, provided the aim is generalizable knowledge rather than purely internal operational troubleshooting for the state. If the work is genuinely only the latter, say so and let the IRB make the call; do not decide it yourself.
* *Human subjects?* Yes, unambiguously. Direct interaction plus collection of PII satisfies both paths.
* *UChicago engaged?* Yes.

Three additional considerations this scenario raises. Water quality results are not a biospecimen and do not change the human subjects determination, but they do raise a disclosure question: if you find contamination, what do you owe the household, and what does your protocol commit you to doing? Working with a state government usually requires local approvals in addition to UChicago's, and local review is what catches context-specific risks that a Chicago board will not see. And because pump operators are identifiable by role in a small area, findings that describe operational failures could expose individuals professionally even without names attached.
</details>

## Principles underneath all of this

Regulatory criteria exist to operationalize three principles from [the Belmont Report](https://www.hhs.gov/ohrp/regulations-and-policy/belmont-report/index.html), and coming back to the principles is what helps when the rules do not obviously cover your situation.


**Respect for persons.** People decide for themselves whether to participate, on the basis of adequate information, free of pressure. Consent is a process, not a signature line.

**Beneficence.** Do not simply avoid harm; maximize possible benefits and minimize possible harms. This includes risks to enumerators, not just respondents.

**Justice.** Do not deny a benefit, or impose a burden, without good reason. Who bears the cost of the research, and who stands to gain from it, is a question about the design and not only about the consent form.

## Practices that make protocols better

These are habits of writing rather than requirements, and they shorten review cycles.

| Practice | What it means |
|---|---|
| **Answer the question and just the question** | IRB questions are narrower than they look. Extra material can invite questions about issues you did not need to raise. |
| **Be precise and clear** | Write for a reviewer who does not know your field, context, or acronyms. Vague procedures can look unplanned. |
| **Phase the submission** | When a project has distinct stages, submit them in phases so each stage can be reviewed based on what is known. |
| **Be specific enough** | Describe procedures accurately without committing to details that may later change and require an amendment. |
